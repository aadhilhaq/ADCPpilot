A site between two chains

A box drawn around an interface groove usually covers the chain on the far side. Whether to load that chain is the one choice the tool refuses to make for you, because both answers are right in different experiments.

Why it happens

The box is a rectangle built around the residues you named. A groove at an interface has its far wall on the other chain, a few Ångstrom away, so the rectangle reaches it. Nothing is wrong: the page is telling you that part of the box is currently empty space where a protein is.

Two numbers are given because they answer different questions. The atoms in the box tell you how much of the wall you are leaving out. The atoms in the chain tell you what you would be loading, since including a chain loads all of it. Half a chain is not a molecule.

A chain is only reported when it has at least about two residues' worth of atoms in the box, so a rectangle clipping one stray side chain of a distant chain does not raise the question.

When to leave it out

Leave the box unticked when the other chain is the partner your peptide is meant to displace. This is the usual case in binder design, and it is the case in the example run.

1YCR is MDM2 with the p53 peptide bound. Chain A is 85 residues of MDM2 and chain B is the 13 residue peptide. The site is the groove that peptide sits in, so a box around the groove reaches chain B almost entirely. Loading it would dock every candidate against the molecule you are trying to beat: the groove would be full, and the scores would be measuring something else. So the example keeps chain A, drops chain B, and leaves the tick box alone.

The same holds for a structure solved with an inhibitor peptide, a prodomain, or any partner whose place you want your peptide to take.

When to include it

Tick the box when the other chain is the far wall of the same groove: a dimer whose binding site lies across the interface, a heterodimeric receptor whose pocket is formed by both subunits, a coiled coil your peptide binds along.

Without it, the peptide is docked against a hole. Poses will fill the empty space, the buried surface area will be wrong, and the energies will reward a conformation that cannot exist once the real protein is there.

The decision changes every number in the run. The grid maps are built after the chains are chosen, so this is not a view setting. A run made with the chain and a run made without it are two different experiments and their energies are not comparable.

How to decide

  1. Draw the box and look at it. Rotate until you can see the groove from the side. If the other chain lines the groove, it is the wall; if it sits in the groove, it is the competitor.
  2. Read the two atom counts. A chain with a few hundred atoms in the box is lining it. A chain with a handful is clipped by the corner of the rectangle and is probably irrelevant either way.
  3. Ask what the peptide is for. To displace that chain, leave it out. To bind a surface that chain helps to form, include it.
  4. If the answer is genuinely unclear, run both and compare. It is two runs rather than one, and the comparison is itself the result: a peptide that only scores well against the hole was scoring the hole.

Narrowing the box instead

Sometimes the right answer is neither. If the other chain is in the box only because the box is larger than the site needs, the better fix is a smaller box: drop the site residues at the far edge, or lower the padding, and check again. A box that covers your groove and nothing else makes the question go away and gives the search less empty space to explore.

From the command line the same choice is --box-chains, which takes ignore to report and load nothing, include to load every chain really in the box, or a list such as B,C to load exactly those. Whichever you choose is recorded in the run's job.json, and the preparation log names the chains it found and what it did about them.
