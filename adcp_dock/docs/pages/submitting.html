Resources and submitting

What to ask the queue for, at the bottom of the Docking card, then what to call the run, where to put it and the options your own cluster requires, in the Submit card. Then press the button.

Let it work the estimate out

Estimate & fill reads your peptides, your search settings and the receptor, works out how long the longest task will take, and fills in the cores and the walltime for you. The line beside it says what it assumed: the stages in the order they will queue, the peptide it sized for, how many waves the list runs in, and how long the whole thing should take end to end. It counts what the settings cost as well: the receptor gradient, AutoSite 1.1 and a box from an AutoSite pocket in the prepare job, and a ring, helical starts, more poses and kept trajectories in each docking task. Each peptide is priced with its own ring, so when the rows differ the line names the peptide that takes longest to dock and its ring, which can be a shorter peptide closed head to tail. The prepare job is booked from the same estimate, and never for less than the four hours it was always given.

The line then adds up what the jobs will book, one after another: the prepare job, the docking tasks, and the hour the report job is given. The docking tasks run side by side, so they count once, unless a limit on tasks at once makes them take turns, when they count once for each wave. That total is the most queue time the run can hold from start to finish.

The booking is deliberately generous. Each job is booked for twice its own estimate and half an hour more, and never for less than two hours. A job the scheduler stops at its limit has produced nothing and has already spent its time in the queue, so an over-booking is the cheaper mistake. Clusters charge for the time used, not the time booked.

Cores per task

Each peptide runs as one task with this many cores, and ADCP spreads its replicas across them. More cores are faster until there are more cores than replicas, after which the extra ones have nothing to do.

Asking for a whole node usually means a longer wait in the queue; half a node often starts and finishes sooner. If you set fewer replicas than cores, lower the cores to match.

Walltime

The scheduler stops the job at this limit, finished or not. The estimate beside the box already includes its margin, so take what the estimate gives you unless you know something it does not.

If your cluster refuses the job because the walltime is longer than the partition allows, the Runs card says so in those words rather than leaving the job pending forever. Lower it, or name a partition that permits it under other Slurm options.

Most tasks at once

Limits how many peptides run at the same time, however many are queued. Empty, or zero, means no limit.

Use it on a busy cluster, or to keep a large screen from taking your whole allocation. It controls concurrency within the array, not the size of the array: a site that refuses an array of 500 tasks still refuses it.

What to call this run

The name becomes the run directory and the name of every Slurm job, so it is what you see in the queue when several runs are going at once. Use something that tells runs apart, such as the receptor and what you are trying, rather than test2.

Where the run is written

Everything the run produces goes here: the prepared receptor, the grid maps, the poses, the energies, the report and the logs. On a cluster it has to be somewhere the compute nodes can see, which a scratch directory is and your laptop is not.

Runs are never overwritten: the run directory is the name you give followed by the date and time, so a second run with the same name sits beside the first rather than on top of it. Browse walks the filesystem and can make a folder.

Keep runs off your home directory. A run holds the grid maps and every pose, and clusters usually give home a small file allowance. $SCRATCH is the right place, and the default is already there when your site sets it.

The allocation to charge

Leave it empty on clusters that charge your default account. Many clusters refuse a job that does not name one, which the page reports as the account on the job is not one you can use.

Enter the account exactly as your cluster gives it. On Slurm, sacctmgr show assoc user=$USER lists the ones you may use. It is added to every job the run submits.

Anything else this cluster requires

Slurm options your site needs that this form does not ask about, each written as --name=value and separated by spaces:

--qos=normal
--partition=short
--constraint=icelake

They are added to every job the run submits. The options the tool sets itself, the name, the log file, the cores, the memory and the time, cannot be overridden here, because the page reads them back afterwards and two of any of them would put the log where it does not look. Anything that could end the line or start a command is refused rather than escaped, with the offending word named.

What submitting does

Three jobs are submitted, each waiting on the one before:

  1. prepare, one job: the receptor preparation, the PDBQT and the grid maps over your box.
  2. dock, one array task per peptide: the search, and the MM-GBSA of its kept poses if you asked for that. The tasks are independent, so one failing does not lose the others.
  3. report, one job: it gathers the scores into the tables.

Every job writes its log into the run's logs folder, named after the stage. The settings you submitted are written as job.json in the run, which is what makes a run reproducible and what reuse settings reads back into the form.

Nothing heavy runs in the page itself. The server reads files, checks what you typed and hands the work to Slurm, so you can close the browser and come back to the Runs card later. On one machine the same three stages run here, in order, one at a time.

Check the site and the box before you submit. Preparation and map building take the same time whether the box covers your groove or half the protein, and the search costs the same whether or not the peptide can fit in the box. Both are a minute's work to check and a wasted allocation to get wrong.
