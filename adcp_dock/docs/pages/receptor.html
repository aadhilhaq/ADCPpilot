The receptor

Load the protein you are docking into, keep the chains you want, and let the preparation turn a crystal structure into something a force field will accept.

Loading a structure

There are four ways in, and they end in the same place.

  • PDB id. Four characters, such as 1YCR. The file is fetched from the RCSB and cached, so each id is downloaded once per machine. This needs the internet, which a compute node usually does not have.
  • A file already on this machine. A .pdb, .cif, .mmcif or .ent file, gzipped or not. Nothing is copied: the path is read where it is, so it must be somewhere the compute nodes can see too. Browse walks the cluster's folders.
  • From this computer. Reads a file on the machine your browser is on and sends it through the connection that is already open. Use this when the cluster cannot see your file.
  • A target file made in AGFR or AGFRGUI (.trg). Its receptor, maps and box are used as they are, and none of the preparation below runs. Do not rename the file: ADCP finds the maps by its name.

An mmCIF is converted to PDB with OpenMM before anything else, because every later step reads fixed-column PDB.

Once a structure is loaded, the chain boxes appear. Tick the chains you want to dock against; none ticked keeps them all. A structure solved with its partner bound, as 1YCR is, usually wants only the receptor chain: see A site between two chains.

A backbone trace is not prepared. One atom per residue means every side chain is missing, and rebuilding them all is modelling rather than preparation. The preparation stops with that message at the start of the run.

What preparation does

The preparation runs as the first job of the run, not while you are typing. Every step is logged in receptor/preparation.log inside the run, and the results page reads that log back, so a step that was skipped is visible afterwards. In order:

  1. Bibliographic header records are dropped, because later steps trip over them.
  2. The chains you kept are kept and the rest dropped.
  3. One conformer per atom where there are alternate locations: the highest occupancy, ties to the first. Docking against both copies is docking against a protein that does not exist.
  4. Modified residues that stand in for an ordinary one are read as that one: selenomethionine as methionine, phosphoserine as serine, and so on.
  5. Waters are removed.
  6. Other heteroatoms are removed unless you keep them. See heteroatoms.
  7. Disulphides within 2.5 Å are found and named in the log, so you can see which cysteines are paired. pdb2pqr works them out for itself.
  8. Missing side-chain atoms are rebuilt and the termini capped, so a chain break is not read as a charged terminus. Whole missing residues are left alone unless you ask for them: see loops.
  9. Hydrogens and titration states at your pH: see protonation.
  10. A restrained minimisation: see minimisation.

Then prepare_receptor writes the PDBQT and agfr builds the grid maps over your box. The example's log reads, in part, kept chain A, dropped B, rebuilt 0 side-chain atom(s), completed 1 terminal atom(s); LEFT 24 missing residue(s) alone and 743 hydrogens added, which is the kind of summary to read before trusting a run.

Protonation and pH

Hydrogens are placed by pdb2pqr, and PROPKA decides each titratable residue's state at the pH you set. That fixes the charges the docking sees: whether a histidine is neutral or positive, whether an aspartate is charged.

7.4 is physiological and is the default. If your assay runs somewhere else, use that pH: an endosomal target at 5.5 presents a different surface. There is no per-residue control here, and the same pH is used again when a complex is built for MM-GBSA, so the docking and the rescoring agree with each other. What that means at a site with histidines in it is worth a page of its own: Histidines at the site.

Minimising after protonation

Minimise the receptor after protonation runs a restrained minimisation: the heavy atoms are held near their measured positions at 10 kcal/mol/Å² while the new hydrogens and any clashes relax.

Leave it on. Hydrogens are placed by geometry and have not been relaxed against the force field, so docking without this docks into strain that is not really there. It takes under a minute for a few hundred residues. In the example it moved the heavy atoms 0.11 Å RMSD and took the energy from -2,620 to -3,295 kcal/mol, which is what a working restrained minimisation looks like: a large energy change and almost no movement.

Heteroatoms

Cofactors, ligands, metals and everything else that is not protein are removed unless you tick Keep heteroatoms.

Tick it when what is bound there is part of the site: a structural zinc, or a haem your peptide has to sit against. Leave it off for a crystallisation additive, or for a ligand you intend to displace.

Keeping heteroatoms skips the minimisation. The force field has no parameters for most of what this keeps, metal ions included, so the minimisation cannot run and the results page says so. You are choosing between a site that is complete and a structure that has been relaxed.

Rebuilding missing loops

Missing side-chain atoms are always rebuilt. Rebuild missing loops is about whole missing residues, the stretches the structure never resolved. It is off by default.

A rebuilt loop is a model, and once it is written into the file it looks exactly like a measured one. Turn it on only when a gap sits against your site and leaving a hole there would be worse than filling it. The example leaves 24 missing residues alone, in two stretches well away from the p53 groove, which is the ordinary case.
