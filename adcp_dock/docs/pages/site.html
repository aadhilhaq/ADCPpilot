The site and the box

Mark where a binder must go: residues you pick, a ligand or peptide already in the structure, or a pocket AutoSite finds. The box is built around it, and ADCP searches inside that box and nowhere else.

Where the box goes

Build the box around offers three ways to place it. Whichever you choose, the box spans what marks the site plus the padding on each side, and is held between the minimum and the maximum.

ChoiceThe box spansThe site each pose is measured against
residues you pickthe residues, plus the paddingthose residues
a ligand or peptide in the structurethe ligand, plus the padding, as AGFR places a box around a ligandthe residues the ligand touches, within 4 Å
a pocket AutoSite findsthe pocket, plus the padding, as AGFR places a box around AutoSite's pointsthe residues lining the pocket, within 4 Å of its points

Residues you pick is how every run made before the choice existed placed its box, and a run's settings reused from such a run come back that way.

The residues

Type them as CHAIN:NUMBER, for instance A:98, or as bare numbers when the receptor is one chain. A range works too: A:98-112. Or click a residue in the structure to add it and click it again to drop it, or click it in the sequence strip beside it.

They do two things:

  • They place the box, which is built around them and then padded.
  • Every pose is checked against them afterwards, so a peptide that lands beside your residues rather than on them is reported as such. That is the nominated site column in the results.

Pick the residues you believe a binder must touch, not the whole pocket. The box grows to cover whatever you name, and a box that is too big spreads the same search more thinly everywhere.

A residue you type that is not in the structure is named in the note rather than ignored, which catches a chain letter that does not exist and a numbering that is not the one in the file.

Around a ligand or peptide in the structure

When the structure was solved with something bound in the site, that is the most direct way to say where the site is. Once a receptor is loaded, Ligand or peptide lists what it holds that a box could be built around:

  • peptides, the protein chains of up to 50 residues, named by their chain, like B;
  • hetero groups, named CHAIN:NAME:NUMBER, like A:NUT:201. Crystallisation additives such as glycerol and sulfate come last, and waters are not listed.

The box is centred on the middle of the ligand's extent and spans that extent plus the padding on each side, which is how AGFR places a box around a ligand (-b ligand), and then it is held between the minimum and the maximum. The residues the ligand touches, within 4 Å, become the site each pose is measured against, so the results say how much of the crystal ligand's footprint a docked peptide covers.

The ligand itself is never docked into. A peptide's chain is left out of the receptor; ticking that chain among the chains to keep is refused, because the peptide would then fill the site it marks. A hetero group is removed with the other heteroatoms, and with Keep heteroatoms on it would stay, so that is refused too.

Why the box is worked out here and not by AGFR. AGFR 1.1 can pick a ligand out of a PDB itself (--pdbLigand), but only on a receptor it prepares itself, with its own protonation, in place of the preparation this tool does. And a peptide chosen that way stays in its receptor: on 1YCR with --pdbLigand "chain B" the target AGFR built held chain B's 132 atoms, so its maps had the peptide in the site. So the box is worked out here by AGFR's rule and given to AGFR as a box. AGFR's own box for chain B came within half an Ångström of it; the difference is the hydrogens AGFR adds to the ligand before it measures it.

Around an AutoSite pocket

With no ligand in the structure and no residues in mind, AutoSite can find the site. Once the receptor is prepared, on the compute node, AutoSite searches the whole receptor for pockets: clusters of grid points where a carbon, an oxygen acceptor or a hydrogen donor binds well. It ranks them by how many points they hold, how strongly and how buried, and the box goes around the best scoring one (agfr -b fill -p best). Every pocket it finds puts one box around all of them (-p all), which on a whole protein is close to docking blind. The residues lining the pocket, within 4 Å of its points, become the site.

The pocket is found on the prepared receptor, so there is nothing to draw before the run. Afterwards, load the run's own target file, target/receptor.trg in its folder, in the target file box of the Receptor card: the site view then shows the receptor, the box and the AutoSite points in pink, and the run's job.json lists every pocket AutoSite found with its score.

The maps for a pocket are built twice. agfr builds the maps for -b fill at the 1 Å spacing AutoSite searched at, which is too coarse to dock into, so the box it chose is kept and the maps are built over it again at the usual 0.375 Å. On MDM2 the best scoring pocket was the p53 groove: 93% of its points lie within 4 Å of the crystal peptide.

The size of the box

Along each axis the box spans the residues, the ligand or the pocket, plus the padding on each side, then it is clamped between the minimum and the maximum. The defaults are 8 Å of padding, a 20 Å minimum and a 30 Å maximum.

SettingWhat it doesWhat goes wrong without it
PaddingHow far the box reaches beyond your residues, ligand or pocket. 8 Å leaves room for a peptide to lie across the site.Below about 5 Å, a longer peptide has nowhere to put its ends.
Min boxThe smallest the box may be.Two or three residues close together would give a box barely larger than they are, which a peptide cannot enter from any direction.
Max boxThe largest the box may be.The search is spread over the whole box, so a wider box does not cost more time; it searches everywhere less thoroughly.

A peptide needs roughly 1.1 Å of box per residue plus about 8 Å, so a 13-mer wants about 23 Å and up to 29 Å if it binds extended. The page works this out from your longest peptide and says so before you submit, and the run says it again in its log. If your residues span more than the maximum, they are probably two sites and are better docked as two runs.

A site larger than the maximum is clipped. The run reports which of your residues the box does not contain and by how much. In the example, three of the 22 residues fall outside the 30 Å box, by 2.7, 1.3 and 0.6 Å. No pose can reach them, and they still count in the site coverage column, so read that column knowing it.

Seeing the box

Show the docking box draws the cage in the structure above. This changes only what you see; it has no effect on the docking.

It is worth a look before every submission. If the box misses part of the site, add a residue there or raise the padding. If it covers far too much, lower the padding or the maximum. A box that is mostly solvent is a search spent mostly on solvent.

The surface

Show the surface draws the molecular surface of the chains you keep: the surface a probe of 1.5 Å, a water molecule, traces as it rolls over the atoms. A groove shows as a groove, which a cartoon does not show. Waters are left out, and hetero groups too unless Keep heteroatoms is ticked, so the surface is the receptor the run will dock into.

It is computed by MSMS, Michel Sanner's program, which ships with ADFRsuite. Each atom is given the radius MSMS's own converter, pdb_to_xyzr, gives it, and the surface is finer for a small receptor than for a large one. MDM2 takes a tenth of a second; GroEL with GroES, 58,870 atoms, takes a few. The surface is kept in the runs folder, so a structure is computed once. Where MSMS is not installed or cannot triangulate the structure, the viewer draws its own surface instead, and a line under the view says why.

This changes only what you see; it has no effect on the docking.

Other chains the box reaches

A box drawn around an interface groove routinely covers the chain on the far side. When that happens the page says so and offers a tick box, because neither answer is right in general.

  • Tick it when that chain is the far wall of the same groove. Without it the peptide is docked against a gap where the wall should be.
  • Leave it unticked when that chain is the partner your peptide is meant to displace. Docking against it would be docking against what you want removed.

Ticking loads the whole chain, not only the part inside the box, because half a chain is not a molecule. The grid maps are then built with it in place, so the decision changes the energies of every pose. A site between two chains works through both answers on one structure.

The grid maps

ADCP does not dock into the receptor itself but into grid maps of it: for each atom type, the energy an atom of that type would have at each point of the box, 0.375 Å apart. AGFR builds them. Two AGFR programs can:

  • AGFR 1.1, which comes with the ADCP 1.1 suite. AGFR, newest installed uses it wherever that suite is installed.
  • AGFR 1.0, which comes with ADFRsuite, and which every run made before this choice used.

Over the MDM2 example's receptor and box, with the same settings, the two wrote the same box, the same receptor and the same starting points. Their maps differ slightly, because each runs its own build of AutoGrid: where a peptide atom can sit, by at most 0.15 kcal/mol in the atom type maps and by up to 1.2 in the hydrogen bond donor maps, with correlations of 0.998 and above. AGFR 1.1 also writes a constriction map, which ADCP does not read. ADCP 1.0 and ADCP 1.1 both dock into maps from either.

The run records which AGFR built its maps, with the command it ran, in job.json under maps. A run made before the choice existed is rebuilt, if it ever needs to be, the way it was first built.

AutoSite

AutoSite marks the places in the box where a ligand is likely to bind: clusters of grid points where a carbon, an oxygen acceptor or a hydrogen donor binds well. ADCP's search starts its replicas from them, and for a box from a pocket they are the pocket.

AutoSite 1.0 keeps only the grid points that bind most strongly, so it tends to miss the weakly bound parts of a large ligand such as a peptide. AutoSite 1.1 relaxes those cutoffs and scans several, which is why the ADCP authors recommend it. It is the default here. It changes where the search starts, not the maps: on the MDM2 example the maps were identical either way, and AutoSite 1.0 placed 380 points where 1.1 placed 246.

The receptor gradient

Inside the receptor no atom can be, and the maps hold very high values there. The gradient replaces them with values that slope toward the receptor's surface, so a peptide atom caught inside is pushed out rather than stuck. Where a peptide atom can actually sit the maps are unchanged: on the MDM2 example they were the same to the last digit with and without it.

It is off unless you tick Add the receptor gradient, because the ADCP authors found it expensive and of no real help to the search. AGFR 1.0 adds it unless told not to, so every run made before this choice has it. On the MDM2 example it took AGFR 1.0 two and a half minutes, against 14 seconds without it, and AGFR 1.1 23 seconds more. The walltime estimate prices it either way.
