Modified and D amino acids

With ADCP 1.1 installed you can dock a phosphoserine, a citrulline, a D residue or any of 197 non-standard side chains, either by typing them or by picking them from a list.

How a modified residue is written

A non-standard residue is written as the one-letter code of the standard residue whose coarse model ADCP uses for it, followed by the library name in angle brackets. A D residue is written with & before its letter.

N<BHD>PIS&DVD        seven residues: a modified asparagine
                       first, and a D-aspartate fifth
S<SEP>                 phosphoserine, modelled as serine
Y<PTR>                 phosphotyrosine
R<CIR>                 citrulline
&K                     D-lysine

Residues are counted as residues, not as characters: N<BHD>PIS&DVD is seven, and the 5 to 30 limit applies to that count. The sequence box counts the same way, typed or chosen: its numbers above the letters go up by one for N<BHD> and for &D, each is highlighted as one residue, and the residue count under the box agrees with them. The library name is matched as written, in capitals; the standard letters are not.

The library is ADCP 1.1's own, built by the Scripps Research Institute from the SwissSidechain database. It holds 209 non-natural side chains, each in an L and a D form, among them phosphorylated serine, threonine and tyrosine, oxidised methionine and tryptophan, citrulline and nitrotyrosine, and many synthetic residues. The 197 that ADCP gives a coarse potential can be written in this syntax, and those are the ones the page offers.

Some obvious modifications are not in it. Lysine acetylation and lysine methylation are not in the library, so they cannot be docked this way. The dialog lists exactly what the installed ADCP 1.1 contains, which is the only reliable answer.

Choosing them in the page

  1. Open the peptide's ⋮ menu and choose Non-standard and D amino acids.
  2. Click the residue you want to change. The sequence is shown with every position clickable.
  3. Pick its D form, or one of the non-standard residues ADCP has for that position.
  4. Each choice is drawn below the list as the residue sits in the chain, with the charge ADCP models it with. That is how you tell SEP from SEP2, phosphoserine at charge -1 and at charge -2.
  5. Save writes the sequence in the notation above. Every residue you changed must have a type chosen before it will save.

A D residue is drawn as the mirror image of the L one, which is how ADCP builds it. The list only ever offers what the installed library contains, so a name that cannot be docked cannot be chosen. Typing one by hand that is not in the library is refused by name.

What changes in the run

Docking happens as usual, with ADCP 1.1 and its side-chain library. Two things are different afterwards.

MM-GBSA is skipped for these peptides. The Amber topology is built from standard residues and the force field has no parameters for the others. Those rows carry no MM-GBSA value, the table lists them as unranked, and the reason is printed. In a list that mixes standard and modified peptides, the standard ones are rescored and ranked and the modified ones are not, so the two groups cannot be ranked against each other on energy.

Use ADCP 1.1's own minimisation instead. Poses to minimize with OpenMM relaxes the top poses with the Amber ff99SB force field and the GBn2 solvent model, holding the receptor away from the peptide in place, and reports the lowest E(complex) minus E(receptor) beside the scores. It is at most five minimiser iterations, ADCP's default, so it relaxes each complex rather than refining it. It is reported and never ranked on.

The minimisation covers nearly every residue the dialog offers, with known exceptions. ADCP 1.1.21 has no parameters for IYR, PBF, PHI and TYI, and its parameters for FVAL, FPG2, FPG3, FTR and FT6 fail in OpenMM. A peptide containing one of these is still docked, its poses are left unminimised, and the run's log says so.

How far to trust it

ADCP builds and docks every residue in the library, and its accuracy on peptides containing them has not been benchmarked in a publication. Treat such results as exploratory: good for choosing which modification to make next, not for a number in a table of affinities.

What is still solid is the comparison between a modified peptide and its own parent sequence in the same run, on the same receptor, with the same settings. That is the experiment worth doing: dock TSFAEYWNLLS and TS<...> variants of it together, look at the poses, and see whether the modification keeps the anchor residues in the groove.

Runs that use these residues also cite SwissSidechain, and GAFF when the poses were minimised. How to cite adds them for you, scoped to the run, so the methods section says what was actually used.
