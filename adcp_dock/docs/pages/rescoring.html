Rescoring with MM-GBSA

A second, physics-based estimate of each pose, computed with AmberTools. It is what makes different peptides comparable, and it is the slowest part of a run.

What it does

ADCP's score is a docking score. It orders the poses of one peptide well, and it was tuned for that rather than for comparing constructs of different length and composition, which is usually what a screen is asking. MM-GBSA computes an interaction energy from a force field with an implicit solvent term instead, so agreement between the two is worth more than either alone.

For each pose that is rescored:

  1. The complex is protonated with the peptide bound, at the run's pH.
  2. tleap builds Amber topologies for the complex, the receptor and the peptide, with the ff14SB protein force field.
  3. sander minimises the complex with the receptor's heavy atoms restrained, up to 1,000 cycles.
  4. MMPBSA.py computes the interaction energy and its four parts on that minimised geometry, with 0.15 M salt.

It roughly doubles the length of a run. It needs AmberTools; without it the option is switched off and the page says why.

It is a ranking aid, not a binding free energy. One conformation, no ensemble average and no entropy term. It gives an ordering within one run against one receptor. It does not give a binding constant, and values from two different receptors are not comparable.

Peptides with non-standard or D residues are not rescored: the topology is built from standard residues and the force field has no parameters for the others. Those rows have no MM-GBSA value, the table says so, and ADCP 1.1's own OpenMM minimisation is the number to use for them instead.

Poses to rescore

MM-GBSA runs on this many of each peptide's poses, best first. The cost grows in proportion and it is the largest cost of the run.

More than one is worth having. A single pose can be lucky, and the spread across poses is what shows whether the peptide has settled on one way of binding or is still choosing. The results table reports that spread, and for the three peptides in the example run it is 27.0, 14.0 and 22.3 kcal/mol across five poses each, which is as large as the differences between the peptides themselves. That is a property of the poses, not of the rescoring, and it is the single most important thing to know before quoting one of these numbers.

The poses are rescored several at a time, as many as the task has cores up to eight, so the wall time is the number of waves rather than the number of poses.

Averaging the best few

Report the mean of the top decides the headline number: the mean of that many best pose energies rather than the single best. The default is 5.

A best score is the tail of a distribution the search drew many times, and it rewards the luckiest pose. The mean of the top few is steadier. Both are written out, since comparison with other work often needs the best as well, along with the median and the spread. Set it to 1 to report the best pose alone.

The ranking uses the mean where MM-GBSA ran, and ADCP's own mean where it did not. The table says which, in the heading of the column it ranked on.

The solvation model

Two implicit solvent models are offered. GB-OBC(II) is the default and is what every earlier run used; GBn2 is a newer model with its own atomic radii. Each is run with the radii it was fitted with, and the run records which, because an energy computed with one set of radii and labelled with the other is wrong in a way nothing downstream can detect.

The two give different numbers. Compare peptides within one model, never across the two.

Each engine writes its own columns, none is merged into another, and the table says which force field and solvent model produced each one. A run may carry all three, and they are read down a column rather than across a row.

A finished run can be scored again with another engine from its own page: nothing is docked again, the same poses are scored, the new numbers appear beside the old ones, and the table stays ranked on the engine it already used.

The OpenMM engine deliberately scores sander's geometry rather than making its own, and declines to run where sander is absent. Letting it minimise further moves the energy by tens of kcal/mol, because the extra relaxation loosens the interface: the two energy models agree, and the minimisation was the whole of the difference.

What else it measures

While each pose is rescored, the run also measures the interface on the same minimised geometry: buried surface area, how much of the peptide is buried, typed contacts and clashes, and the per residue breakdown of the energy. Those are the interface columns in the results, and they are described in Reading the results.
