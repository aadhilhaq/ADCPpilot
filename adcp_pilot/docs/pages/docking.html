Docking settings

How hard to search, and how much of the answer to keep. These three settings decide whether a result is reproducible, and they are the ones most often left at a default that no published work uses.

Search replicas

ADCP runs this many independent searches from different random starts and keeps the best. They are the main protection against one search settling in the wrong place.

The authors' guideline is 100 to 300:

"A guideline should be running 100-300 replicas with a maximum Monte Carlo
 steps of 1-3 million per amino acid in the peptide."
                               (ccsb.scripps.edu/adcp/documentation)

The v1.1 paper used 300, the original paper 80, and the redocking tutorial suggests 100. ADCP's own default is 50, below all of them. The dropdown says where each choice sits:

ReplicasWhat it is

50ADCP's default, below the 100 to 300 guideline

400above the v1.1 paper's 300

The cost is not linear. Replicas run across the cores the task holds, so up to the core count extra replicas add little wall time: 100 replicas on 48 cores run in three waves, the same as 144 would. Fewer replicas than cores leaves cores idle.

Steps per replica

How long each search runs. ADCP's guidance is that this scales with the length of the peptide, 1 to 3 million Monte Carlo steps per amino acid, so 10 to 30 million for a 10-mer. Pick roughly your peptide's length times 1 to 3 million:

ResiduesSteps
55,000,000 to 15,000,000
88,000,000 to 24,000,000
1010,000,000 to 30,000,000
1515,000,000 to 45,000,000

The dropdown offers 100,000 (a smoke test, far below anything published), 500,000, 2,500,000 (ADCP's default, about two residues' worth), 10,000,000 (a 10-mer at the lower bound) and 20,000,000 (a 10-mer at 2 million per residue, or a 20-mer at 1 million).

It is a maximum rather than a fixed cost: a search that converges early stops. Too few steps show up as poses that disagree with each other and as a best score that moves when you repeat the run.

A small search still produces a table. Nothing fails when the settings are too low. The scores come back, the ranking looks the same as any other, and only a repeat run shows that it was noise. Decide the settings before you read the numbers.

Poses per peptide

How many of ADCP's ranked poses to write and keep (ADCP's -m). They are what you look at afterwards and what MM-GBSA rescores, so this sets the size of the answer rather than the size of the search.

Ten is the default and is enough to see whether a peptide's poses agree on where it binds. Keeping one pose hides that question entirely: the results table reports the spread across poses, and with a single pose there is none to report.

ADCP writes as many as you ask for, up to 100, one for each cluster its search found, best first, so a search that found fewer clusters writes fewer. More poses cost a few seconds to write, not a longer search, and the estimate includes them.

Keeping the trajectories

Each replica of the search writes its trajectory, every pose it accepted on the way. ADCP deletes them when the docking ends unless told to keep them (its -k), so a run keeps only its ranked poses, unless Keep every replica's trajectory is ticked.

Ticked, each peptide's trajectories are packed into one archive beside its poses, docking/<peptide>/<peptide>_trajectories.tar.gz, and the loose files are removed: for a 50 replica run, about 150 files become one, which matters on a cluster that limits the number of files as well as their size. Two copies are left out of the archive: the maps ADCP unpacked from the target, and out.pdb, which is every trajectory again in one file. ADCP writes both again when the archive is unpacked in the peptide's folder and it is asked to recluster (-r).

Packing adds a few seconds to each peptide and the archive is tens of megabytes, so leave it off unless you will look at the trajectories. A run that stops part way through packing finishes the archive when it is resumed, and a peptide whose archive is complete is not docked again.

The engine, and the minimization

Docking engine shows the ADCP installed here, ADCP 1.1 with its version, which every run docks with. It docks non-standard and D amino acids and can minimize the top poses with OpenMM; see Tools. Without the suite the line under it says nothing can dock, and the run cannot be submitted.

On a Mac, This computer docks with ADCP 1.0 (macOS) instead, because Scripps builds no ADCP 1.1 for macOS, and the line under it says that its scores are not comparable with runs docked with ADCP 1.1. There, non-standard and D amino acids and the OpenMM minimization are not offered, and a peptide gets at most 10 poses. See On a Mac.

Poses to minimize with OpenMM is ADCP's own step. It minimizes that many of the top poses in implicit solvent and reports the lowest E(complex) minus E(receptor) beside the scores. ADCP holds the receptor residues away from the peptide in place and runs at most five minimizer iterations, its default, so this relaxes each complex rather than refining it. The number is reported and never ranked on.

The panel beside every setting

The panel carries the published guidance where there is any, with the source, which is the part that is hard to find again six months later when you are writing the methods section. It is the same text wherever the setting appears, and it is the text this documentation is written against.

The pH and the receptor's other settings

The pH, and the switches for heteroatoms, side chains, loops and the minimization, are not in this card. They decide the receptor rather than the search, so they are in the Receptor card under the chains they apply to, in the order they are applied. What each does is covered in what preparation does.
