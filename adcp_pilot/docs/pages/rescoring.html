Rescoring with MM-GBSA

A second, physics-based estimate of each pose, computed with AmberTools. It is what makes different peptides comparable, and it is the slowest part of a run.

What it does

ADCP's score is a docking score. It orders the poses of one peptide well, and it was tuned for that rather than for comparing constructs of different length and composition, which is usually what a screen is asking. MM-GBSA computes an interaction energy from a force field with an implicit solvent term instead, so agreement between the two is worth more than either alone.

For each pose that is rescored:

  1. The complex is protonated with the peptide bound, at the run's pH.
  2. tleap builds Amber topologies for the complex, the receptor and the peptide, with the ff14SB protein force field. The peptide is written as a chain of its own, B unless the receptor uses B and then the first letter it does not use, and a TER record ends the receptor, each of its chains and each piece of a chain broken by a gap, so nothing is bonded that is not bonded in the structure.
  3. sander minimizes the complex with the receptor's heavy atoms restrained, up to 1,000 cycles.
  4. MMPBSA.py computes the interaction energy and its four parts on that minimized geometry, with 0.15 M salt.

It roughly doubles the length of a run. It needs AmberTools; without it the option is switched off and the page says why.

It is a ranking aid, not a binding free energy. One conformation, no ensemble average and no entropy term. It gives an ordering within one run against one receptor. It does not give a binding constant, and values from two different receptors are not comparable.

Peptides with D residues, and rings whose pose is closed, are rescored and ranked with the rest: see D residues and rings. Peptides with non-standard residues are not: the force field has no parameters for their side chains. Those rows have no MM-GBSA value, the table lists them as unranked with the reason, and ADCP 1.1's own OpenMM minimization is the number to use for them instead.

Poses to rescore

MM-GBSA runs on this many of each peptide's poses, best first. The cost grows in proportion and it is the largest cost of the run.

More than one is worth having. A single pose can be lucky, and the spread across poses is what shows whether the peptide has settled on one way of binding or is still choosing. The results table reports that spread, and for the three peptides in the example run it is 20.6, 16.2 and 15.5 kcal/mol across five poses each, which is as large as the differences between the peptides themselves. That is a property of the poses, not of the rescoring, and it is the single most important thing to know before quoting one of these numbers.

The poses are rescored several at a time, as many as the task has cores up to eight, so the wall time is the number of waves rather than the number of poses.

Averaging the best few

Report the mean of the top decides the headline number: the mean of that many best pose energies rather than the single best. The default is 5.

A best score is the tail of a distribution the search drew many times, and it rewards the luckiest pose. The mean of the top few is steadier. Both are written out, since comparison with other work often needs the best as well, along with the median and the spread. Set it to 1 to report the best pose alone.

The ranking uses the mean where MM-GBSA ran, and ADCP's own mean where it did not. The table says which, in the heading of the column it ranked on.

The solvation model

Two implicit solvent models are offered. GB-OBC(II) is the default and is what every earlier run used; GBn2 is a newer model with its own atomic radii. Each is run with the radii it was fitted with, and the run records which, because an energy computed with one set of radii and labeled with the other is wrong in a way nothing downstream can detect.

The two give different numbers. Compare peptides within one model, never across the two.

Each engine writes its own columns, none is merged into another, and the table says which force field and solvent model produced each one. A run may carry all three, and they are read down a column rather than across a row.

A finished run can be scored again with another engine through Add measures: nothing is docked again, the same poses are scored, the new numbers appear beside the old ones, and the table stays ranked on the engine it already used.

The OpenMM engine deliberately scores sander's geometry rather than making its own, and declines to run where sander is absent. Letting it minimize further moves the energy by tens of kcal/mol, because the extra relaxation loosens the interface: the two energy models agree, and the minimization was the whole of the difference.

D residues and rings

A D residue is built with the template of its L form, and the docked coordinates carry its handedness. This is exact rather than an approximation, because ff14SB has no term that tells a residue from its mirror image. Every torsion term in it has a phase of 0 or 180 degrees, so it gives the same energy for an angle and for its negative, and it has no CMAP grid, the one protein term that would not. Bonds, angles, van der Waals, electrostatics and the solvation term depend only on distances and angles, which a mirror image keeps. It was checked directly: a D peptide and its exact mirror image as an L peptide have the same energy, term by term. After a minimization of 1,000 steps they still agree within 1 kcal/mol, because the minimizer's path from two mirrored starting points differs by rounding.

The one thing the L template would get wrong is where tleap puts the atoms a docked pose does not carry, the hydrogens and the last residue's second oxygen, which it takes from the template's own geometry: on a D residue it puts the alpha hydrogen on the wrong side. So those atoms of each D residue are placed as the mirror image of where they go on its L form. ADCP 1.1 builds a D threonine or a D isoleucine with only its alpha carbon inverted, which is the allo form, and the hydrogen on its beta carbon is then put on the open side of that carbon. After the minimization every stereocenter of the peptide is checked against the docked pose, and a pose in which any center changed its handedness is not scored.

A ring is rescored only on the poses that close it. ADCP's ring is a restraint its search pulls on rather than a bond, and a pose can leave the two ends apart. A pose counts as closed when the two atoms the closing bond joins are no further apart than this:

The two distances come from ring poses docked into MDM2, each of which was also built with its closing bond and minimized whatever its distance, to see what closing it does. A carbon and a nitrogen that are not bonded touch at about 3.25 Å, so ends within 2.5 Å are held together by ADCP's restraint rather than resting in contact, and the further apart they are, the more the minimization has to move an end residue to close the bond, which can turn its alpha carbon into its mirror image. The sulfurs of a disulfide fell in two groups with ADCP 1.1, 2.0 to 3.2 Å or 3.9 Å and more, nothing in between. Two sulfurs that are not bonded touch at about 3.6 Å, so a pair closer than 3.5 Å is held there by the ring, and 3.5 Å is also where ADCP's own minimization adds the bond. ADCP's head to tail restraint holds the two end residues closer together than a peptide bond would, so the minimization opens the join out a little.

A closed pose is built with its closing bond: a head to tail ring with no free amine and no carboxylate, and a disulfide between two CYX residues. The bond is checked after the minimization as well, and a pose whose bond the minimization did not bring to length is not scored. A pose that is not closed is not rescored, and the task log says so for that pose, for example ring not closed in this pose: ends 4.31 Å apart. A ring is never scored as a linear chain.

The poses considered are the same top poses as for every other peptide. A ring whose third pose is open is scored on its other closed poses, not on a pose further down the list, and the MM n column says how many poses its value is the mean of. A ring none of whose top poses is closed has no MM-GBSA value: it keeps its docking score and its interface panel, and the table lists it as unranked with the reason.

They rank with the others. D peptides and closed rings are scored with the same engine, force field, minimization and solvent model as linear peptides of L residues, so all of them are ranked together in one table.

AmberTools and OpenMM score both, from the same topology. Prime builds its own structure from a PDB file, and nothing has shown that it keeps a closing bond or a D center as they were docked, so it refuses these peptides and says why rather than score another molecule.

ADCP 1.1's own minimized poses are not used. Its OpenMM minimization does add a ring's closing bond, but with another force field, on a receptor it completes itself, and only in a run that asked for it. Taking those poses for rings while linear peptides start from their docked poses would score the two from different starting points, so every peptide is rescored from its docked pose.

A run set up before this version keeps skipping D residues and rings when it is resumed or rescored, so one run never ranks peptides scored under two rules. Start a new run to rescore them.

What else it measures

While each pose is rescored, the run also measures the interface on the same minimized geometry: buried surface area, how much of the peptide is buried, typed contacts and clashes, and the per residue breakdown of the energy. Those are the interface columns in the results, and they are described in Reading the results.

Then it measures the peptide strain of the same poses: the peptide alone in its pose, against the same peptide relaxed on its own from it, in the same solvent model. It is reported beside the energy and never ranked on. adcp-pilot new --no-strain leaves it out of a run.
